## 1. Why Model? The Modelling Hierarchy & Uncertainty

### 1.1 The Role of Models in Control Design

Control design is carried out primarily on a mathematical model rather than directly on physical hardware. Operating directly on physical hardware during early design iterations can be costly, time-consuming, or hazardous. A mathematical model allows engineers to:

1. **Simulate dynamic behaviour** under diverse operating regimes and extreme conditions without risking physical damage.
2. **Synthesise controllers analytically** to meet exact performance and stability specifications before implementation.
3. **Generalise past observations** to predict system responses to previously unencountered inputs and environmental disturbances.

### 1.2 The Hierarchy of System Models

Modelling involves progressing through four distinct levels of abstraction:

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 1. Real Physical System                                               |
|    - Contains full physical complexity, distributed parameters,       |
|      stray parasitics, friction, and environmental noise.             |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
                                   |
                                   v  (Physical Abstraction & Isolation)
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 2. Idealised Physical Model                                           |
|    - Interconnection of lumped, ideal physical components             |
|      (e.g., point masses, linear springs, lumped resistors).          |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
                                   |
                                   v  (Application of Fundamental Physical Laws)
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 3. Mathematical Model                                                 |
|    - High-order, often nonlinear ordinary or partial differential     |
|      equations describing dynamic relationships between variables.    |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
                                   |
                                   v  (Linearisation & Neglect of Parasitics)
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 4. Simplified Linear Model (LTI)                                      |
|    - Linear, constant-coefficient ODEs and Laplace transfer functions  |
|      operating near a nominal operating point.                        |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

### 1.3 Model Uncertainty

A central principle emphasised in control theory is that **"all models are approximations of reality"**. No mathematical model represents the real physical plant with complete precision.

- **Sources of Uncertainty**: Physical parameter drift (e.g., thermal changes in resistance, wear in mechanical dampers), unmodelled high-frequency dynamics (e.g., structural flexibility, sensor delays), and neglected nonlinearities (e.g., Coulomb friction, actuator saturation).
- **Engineering Implication**: A model must capture the _dominant_ dynamics necessary for feedback control while remaining analytically tractable. Feedback controllers must then be designed to remain robust against the inevitable residual model uncertainty.

## 2. Analytical Modelling (First-Principles Derivation)

Analytical modelling relies on established scientific and physical laws to construct differential equations directly from the interconnection of idealised physical components.

### 2.1 Governing Physical Laws Across Engineering Domains

When constructing models from first principles, engineers sum across energy storage and dissipative mechanisms:

- **Mechanical Translational Systems**: Newton’s Second Law of Motion: $$\sum F_{\text{ext}}(t) = m a(t) = m \frac{d^2 x(t)}{dt^2}$$ where forces originate from applied actuators, Hooke's law for springs ($F_k = k x$), and viscous damping forces ($F_b = c \dot{x}$).
- **Mechanical Rotational Systems**: Newton's Rotational Analogue: $$\sum T_{\text{ext}}(t) = J \alpha(t) = J \frac{d^2 \theta(t)}{dt^2}$$ balancing inertia torque $J \ddot{\theta}$, torsional stiffness $k \theta$, and viscous rotational damping $B \dot{\theta}$.
- **Electrical Networks**: Kirchhoff’s Laws:
    - _Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL)_: The algebraic sum of voltages around any closed loop is zero ($\sum V_k = 0$).
    - _Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL)_: The algebraic sum of currents entering a circuit node is zero ($\sum I_k = 0$).

---

### 2.2 Benchmark Analytical Model 1: Translational Mass-Spring-Damper

Consider a classic second-order mechanical system consisting of a rigid mass $m$, a viscous friction damper with damping coefficient $c$, and a linear restoring spring with spring constant $k$, subjected to an external driving force $u(t)$:

```
                    c (Viscous Damper)
              +-------[===]-------+
              |                   |
    Wall |----+                   +----|  Mass  | ----> Position y(t)
         |    |                   |    |   m    |
         |    +-------/\/\/\------+    +--------+ ===> External Force u(t)
                    k (Spring)
```

1. **Free-Body Dynamic Force Balance**: Applying Newton's second law along the horizontal axis: $$u(t) - F_{\text{damper}}(t) - F_{\text{spring}}(t) = m \frac{d^2 y(t)}{dt^2}$$ Substituting the constituent equations $F_{\text{damper}}(t) = c \dot{y}(t)$ and $F_{\text{spring}}(t) = k y(t)$: $$m \frac{d^2 y(t)}{dt^2} + c \frac{dy(t)}{dt} + k y(t) = u(t)$$
    
2. **Laplace Transformation**: Taking the unilateral Laplace transform assuming zero initial conditions ($y(0) = \dot{y}(0) = 0$): $$\left( m s^2 + c s + k \right) Y(s) = U(s)$$
    
3. **Transfer Function Formulation**: $$G(s) = \frac{Y(s)}{U(s)} = \frac{1}{m s^2 + c s + k} = \frac{\frac{1}{m}}{s^2 + \left(\frac{c}{m}\right)s + \left(\frac{k}{m}\right)}$$ Comparing this directly with the standard prototype second-order characteristic denominator $s^2 + 2\zeta\omega_n s + \omega_n^2$ yields the fundamental system parameters: $$\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} \quad \text{(Undamped Natural Frequency)}$$ $$2\zeta\omega_n = \frac{c}{m} \implies \zeta = \frac{c}{2\sqrt{km}} \quad \text{(Damping Ratio)}$$ This analytical model provides immediate physical insights into parameter influences:
    
    - **Increasing mass $m$** decreases $\omega_n$, making the system response slower and more sluggish.
    - **Increasing damping coefficient $c$** increases $\zeta$, attenuating transient oscillations and reducing overshoot.
    - **Increasing stiffness $k$** increases natural frequency $\omega_n$, stiffening the response and speeding up initial movement.

### 2.3 Benchmark Analytical Model 2: Series RLC Circuit

Consider a series electrical network comprising a resistor $R$, an inductor $L$, and a capacitor $C$ driven by an input voltage source $v(t)$, where the output is the capacitor voltage $v_C(t)$:

```
              L (Inductor)      R (Resistor)
          +-----UUUUUU------------/\/\/\-------+
          |                                    |
        + -                                  + -
     v(t) (~)                              v_C(t) [ C (Capacitor) ]
        - +                                  - +
          |                                    |
          +------------------------------------+
```

1. **Integro-Differential Equation via KVL**: Summing the voltage drops around the loop: $$L \frac{di(t)}{dt} + R i(t) + v_C(t) = v(t)$$ Using the capacitor constitutive relation $i(t) = C \frac{dv_C(t)}{dt}$: $$L C \frac{d^2 v_C(t)}{dt^2} + R C \frac{dv_C(t)}{dt} + v_C(t) = v(t)$$
    
2. **Transfer Function**: Applying the Laplace transform with zero initial conditions: $$(L C s^2 + R C s + 1) V_C(s) = V(s)$$ $$G(s) = \frac{V_C(s)}{V(s)} = \frac{\frac{1}{LC}}{s^2 + \left(\frac{R}{L}\right)s + \frac{1}{LC}}$$ Here, the natural frequency and damping ratio map directly to electrical quantities: $$\omega_n = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}, \qquad \zeta = \frac{R}{2}\sqrt{\frac{C}{L}}$$

### 2.4 Energy Storage Elements and Transfer Function Order

A fundamental principle highlighted in Week 2 is the relationship between the physical components and the mathematical order of the model:

- **The Rule of System Order**: The **order of the transfer function** (the highest power of $s$ in the characteristic denominator polynomial) equals the **number of independent energy storage elements** present in the physical system.
- **Energy Storage Elements**:
    - _Translational Mechanical_: Masses (kinetic energy $E_k = \frac{1}{2}m v^2$) and Springs (potential strain energy $E_p = \frac{1}{2}k x^2$).
    - _Rotational Mechanical_: Rotor inertias ($E_k = \frac{1}{2}J \omega^2$) and Torsional shafts ($E_p = \frac{1}{2}K \theta^2$).
    - _Electrical_: Inductors (magnetic field energy $E_m = \frac{1}{2}L i^2$) and Capacitors (electric field energy $E_e = \frac{1}{2}C v^2$).
    - _Thermal & Fluid_: Thermal capacitances (stored heat $m c_p \Delta T$) and Fluid reservoirs/tanks (potential hydrostatic energy).
- **Energy Dissipating Elements**: Dampers (viscous friction) and Resistors do not store energy; they dissipate energy as heat and contribute to the real parts of the system poles (damping) without increasing the system order.

### 2.5 Mechanical–Electrical Physical Analogies (Series / Force–Voltage Analogue)

Because both mechanical and electrical systems obey analogous second-order differential equations, engineers frequently exploit direct mathematical analogies:

|Mechanical Component (Translational)|Constitutive Relation|Electrical Equivalent (Series Mesh)|Constitutive Relation|
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
|**Applied Force $F(t)$**|Driving input|**Voltage $v(t)$**|Driving potential|
|**Velocity $v(t) = \dot{x}(t)$**|Dynamic flow|**Current $i(t)$**|Charge flow|
|**Mass $M$** (Kinetic energy)|$F = M \frac{dv}{dt}$|**Inductance $L$** (Magnetic energy)|$v = L \frac{di}{dt}$|
|**Damper $c$ or $f_v$** (Dissipation)|$F = c v$|**Resistance $R$** (Thermal dissipation)|$v = R i$|
|**Spring $k$** (Potential energy)|$F = k \int v , dt$|**Capacitance $1/C$** (Elastance)|$v = \frac{1}{C}\int i , dt$|

### 2.6 Linearisation Around an Equilibrium Operating Point

Most physical systems exhibit nonlinear dynamics (e.g., aerodynamic drag proportional to $v^2$, gravitational torque on a pendulum $mgl \sin\theta$, or fluid flow through orifices proportional to $\sqrt{\Delta P}$). Linear control techniques (Laplace transforms, frequency response, root locus) require linear time-invariant approximations near a nominal operating point.

#### The Mathematical Linearisation Procedure

1. **Define the Equilibrium Point**: Consider an autonomous or controlled nonlinear differential equation $\dot{x}(t) = f(x(t), u(t))$. An equilibrium operating point $(x_e, u_e)$ satisfies the steady-state zero-derivative condition: $$f(x_e, u_e) = 0$$
2. **Define Deviation Variables (Perturbations)**: Let small perturbations about the equilibrium be defined as: $$\delta x(t) = x(t) - x_e, \qquad \delta u(t) = u(t) - u_e$$
3. **Perform First-Order Taylor Series Expansion**: Expanding $f(x, u)$ around $(x_e, u_e)$: $$\dot{x} = \frac{d(x_e + \delta x)}{dt} = \dot{\delta x} = f(x_e, u_e) + \left. \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \right|_{(x_e, u_e)} \delta x(t) + \left. \frac{\partial f}{\partial u} \right|_{(x_e, u_e)} \delta u(t) + \text{H.O.T.}$$
4. **Neglect Higher-Order Terms (H.O.T.)**: Because $f(x_e, u_e) = 0$, and assuming perturbations $\delta x$ and $\delta u$ remain small, higher-order powers ($\delta x^2, \delta x \delta u$, etc.) are neglected: $$\dot{\delta x}(t) \approx A \delta x(t) + B \delta u(t)$$ where the constant Jacobian matrices are: $$A = \left. \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \right|_{(x_e, u_e)}, \qquad B = \left. \frac{\partial f}{\partial u} \right|_{(x_e, u_e)}$$ Taking the Laplace transform of this perturbation equation yields a valid local LTI transfer function relating $\delta X(s)$ to $\delta U(s)$.

## 3. Empirical Modelling (System Identification from Data)

### 3.1 The "Black-Box" System Identification Paradigm

When systems involve complex, distributed, or poorly understood physics (e.g., biological metabolisms, industrial chemical distillation columns, aerodynamic turbulence, or soft robotics), deriving exact equations from first principles is difficult or impossible.

In such cases, the system is treated as a **"black box"**:

```
                         +-----------------------------+
    Test Input u(t) ---->|      Physical System        |----> Measured Output y(t)
                         |  (Internal physics unknown) |
                         +-----------------------------+
```

By applying known input signals $u(t)$ and logging the resulting output response data $y(t)$, an empirical model is fitted to match the observed input-output behaviour.

---

### 3.2 The Three Systematic Steps of System Identification

System identification follows a three-step methodology:

```
+--------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| STEP 1: CHOOSE A MODEL STRUCTURE                                         |
| - First-order plus delay: G(s) = K / (τs + 1)                            |
| - Second-order underdamped: G(s) = K ωn^2 / (s^2 + 2ζωn s + ωn^2)        |
| - General polynomial rational transfer function                          |
+--------------------------------------------------------------------------+
                                     |
                                     v
+--------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| STEP 2: APPLY INFORMATIVE TEST INPUTS & COLLECT DATA                     |
| - Excite all relevant dynamic modes (Step, Impulse, Sine Sweeps, PRBS)   |
| - Record input-output pairs {u(t_k), y(t_k)} over time interval T        |
+--------------------------------------------------------------------------+
                                     |
                                     v
+--------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| STEP 3: ESTIMATE MODEL PARAMETERS & OPTIMISE                             |
| - Minimize cost function: Least-Squares error criterion                  |
| - Extract parameters graphically or numerically via optimization         |
+--------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

#### Step 1: Choose a Model Structure

The designer decides on the mathematical structure and order of the candidate model prior to fitting:

- **Prior Insight Selection**: If physical insight indicates a single dominant thermal lag or a spring-mass resonance, a specific first-order or second-order structure is chosen.
- **Generic Rational Transfer Function**: $$G(s) = \frac{b_m s^m + b_{m-1} s^{m-1} + \dots + b_0}{s^n + a_{n-1} s^{n-1} + \dots + a_0}$$
- **First-Order Model**: $$G(s) = \frac{K}{\tau s + 1}$$ governed by static DC gain $K$ and time constant $\tau$.

#### Step 2: Apply Informative Test Inputs and Collect Output Data

To identify a model reliably, the test input signal must **persistently excite** the system across the frequency spectrum where control will take place:

- **Step Input ($u(t) = U_0 \cdot \mathbf{1}(t)$)**: The most common industrial test (bump test). Reveals steady-state DC gain, dominant time constants, presence of pure time delays, and oscillatory damping behaviour.
- **Impulse Input**: Provides the system’s natural transient modes directly in the time domain.
- **Sinusoidal Inputs / Sine Frequency Sweeps**: Tests the system at discrete frequencies $\omega$ to generate empirical Bode gain and phase plots directly.

#### Step 3: Estimate Model Parameters (Least-Squares Optimization)

The model parameters (collected in vector $\theta$) are tuned so that the predicted model output $y_\theta(t_k)$ tracks the measured experimental output $y(t_k)$ as closely as possible.

- **Sum of Squared Errors (Least-Squares Criterion)**: The standard optimization problem minimizes the quadratic prediction error over $N$ sampled discrete-time measurements: $$J(\theta) = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \left[ y(t_k) - y_\theta(t_k) \right]^2$$ where:
    - $y(t_k)$ is the true measured output at sampling instant $t_k$.
    - $y_\theta(t_k)$ is the simulated output produced by the candidate model transfer function $G(s; \theta)$ driven by the recorded input $u(t_k)$.
- **Solution Methods**: Linear least-squares (via normal equations) for linear regression structures, or nonlinear least-squares algorithms (e.g., Levenberg-Marquardt, gradient descent) implemented in computational tools like MATLAB’s System Identification Toolbox.

### 3.3 Graphical Parameter Extraction from Step Response Data (FOTD / Bump Test)

For monotone, non-oscillatory processes, empirical models are frequently extracted directly from step response curves. Consider a First-Order with Time Delay (FOTD) model: $$G(s) = \frac{K}{\tau s + 1} e^{-Ls}$$ where $K$ is the DC gain, $\tau$ is the time constant, and $L$ is the apparent physical transport lag/dead time.

```
   y(t) ^
        |                                       +---------------- y_final
        |                                   .-'
        |                                .-'
 0.632K |-----------------------------.-'
        |                           .'|
        |                         .'  |
        |       Tangent Line    .'    |
        |        \            .'      |
        |         \         .'        |
        |          \     _.'          |
        +-----------+---+-+-----------+-------------------------> t
        0           t0  L            T_63
                    <--->
                   Delay L
                    <----------------->
                        Time Constant τ = T_63 - L
```

1. **Steady-State DC Gain ($K$)**: Calculate the ratio of the total change in output to the magnitude of the input step change $\Delta u$: $$K = \frac{\Delta y_{\text{final}}}{\Delta u} = \frac{y(\infty) - y(0)}{u(\infty) - u(0)}$$
2. **Apparent Time Delay ($L$)**: Draw a tangent line through the steepest inflection point of the step response curve. The time coordinate where this tangent intersects the initial baseline defines the apparent dead time $L$.
3. **Time Constant ($\tau$) via the $63.2\%$ Method**: For a first-order system, the output reaches $1 - e^{-1} \approx 63.2\%$ of its net change after one time constant has elapsed past the delay: $$y(T_{63}) - y(0) = 0.632 \cdot \Delta y_{\text{final}} \implies \tau = T_{63} - L$$

### 3.4 Overfitting, Noise Rejection, and Model Validation

#### The Risk of Overfitting

When selecting a model structure, a higher-order model (more poles and zeros) can achieve a smaller fitting error on the training dataset. However, this introduces **overfitting**:

- An overly complex model fits the **high-frequency measurement noise** and transient recording anomalies rather than the underlying physical plant dynamics.
- Such models generalize poorly, resulting in inaccurate predictions and unstable feedback loops when deployed in real-world environments.
- **Rule of Parsimony**: A simpler, lower-order model that captures the dominant time constants and natural frequencies is preferred.

#### Model Validation

A model must never be evaluated solely on the data used to estimate its parameters.

- **Cross-Validation**: Split the collected experimental data into two distinct subsets:
    1. **Estimation (Training) Dataset**: Used to calculate the optimal parameter vector $\theta$.
    2. **Validation (Test) Dataset**: Driven by a different input excitation signal. The model’s simulated output $y_\theta(t)$ is compared against this unseen dataset.
- If the model achieves a high coefficient of determination (fit percentage) on the unseen validation dataset without exhibiting spurious oscillations, it is validated for controller design.

## 4. Analytical vs. Data-Driven Modelling: Comprehensive Comparison

|Characteristic|Analytical Modelling (First-Principles)|Empirical Modelling (System Identification)|
|:--|:--|:--|
|**Foundational Basis**|Universal physical and scientific laws (Newton, Kirchhoff, conservation).|Input-output experimental time/frequency measurements.|
|**System Knowledge Required**|Complete internal physical architecture and parameter specifications.|None; the system can be treated as an unknown "black box".|
|**Parameter Physical Meaning**|High; parameters correspond directly to mass, resistance, volume, etc..|Low; parameters are mathematical polynomial coefficients or fits.|
|**Handling of Unmodelled Physics**|Struggles with complex friction, turbulence, and distributed parasitics.|Naturally captures lumped empirical effects present in the test data.|
|**Risk of Overfitting**|None; physical component laws strictly constrain the model structure.|High; overly high polynomial orders fit noise instead of true dynamics.|
|**Generalisability**|High; can predict behaviour across varying configurations and sizes.|Limited; accurate primarily near the amplitude and frequency ranges tested.|
|**Common Combined Approach**|**Grey-Box Modelling**: Derive the differential equation structure from physics, then use system identification to estimate uncertain numerical parameters.|
