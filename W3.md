## 1. System Stability: BIBO, Internal Stability, and Hidden Modes

### 1.1 Bounded-Input Bounded-Output (BIBO) Stability

A continuous-time linear time-invariant (LTI) system is defined as **BIBO stable** if every bounded input signal produces a bounded output signal: $$\exists , M_u < \infty \text{ such that } |u(t)| \le M_u ; \forall , t \ge 0 \implies \exists , M_y < \infty \text{ such that } |y(t)| \le M_y ; \forall , t \ge 0$$

- **Poles in the Open Left-Half-Plane (OLHP)**: An LTI transfer function $G(s) = \frac{b(s)}{a(s)}$ is BIBO stable **if and only if all poles of $G(s)$ lie strictly in the OLHP** ($\text{Re}(p_i) < 0$).
- **Poles on the Imaginary Axis**: Systems with poles on the imaginary axis ($\text{Re}(p_i) = 0$) are **not** BIBO stable. For example, a pure integrator $G(s) = \frac{1}{s}$ has a pole at $s = 0$. When excited by a bounded unit step input $u(t) = 1(t)$, the resulting output is an unbounded ramp $y(t) = t$, violating BIBO stability. Similarly, simple conjugate poles on the $j\omega$-axis yield an unbounded resonance when excited by a sinusoid matched to that natural frequency.

```
                            jω
                             |
         UNSTABLE REGION     |     UNSTABLE REGION

```

### 1.2 Internal Stability vs. BIBO Stability

In state-space and interconnected control loops, input-output behaviour does not always capture the state of every internal component:

- **Internal (Asymptotic) Stability**: For an unforced linear system $\dot{x}(t) = Ax(t)$, the equilibrium $x = 0$ is internally stable if and only if **all eigenvalues of the system matrix $A$ have strictly negative real parts**. Under this condition, the state vector $x(t)$ decays exponentially to zero from any initial state $x(0)$.
- **The Relationship**: Internal stability **guarantees** BIBO stability. However, BIBO stability does **not** necessarily guarantee internal stability if pole-zero cancellations occur between open-loop components.

### 1.3 The Danger of Unstable Pole-Zero Cancellations

A critical design rule in feedback engineering is that **an unstable plant pole must never be cancelled by a controller zero**.

Consider a plant with an unstable pole at $s = +1$ and a stabilizing controller attempting to cancel it: $$P(s) = \frac{s - 1}{(s + 3)(s - 1)}, \qquad C(s) = 1$$

Algebraically cancelling $(s - 1)$ yields the reference-to-output transfer function: $$G(s) = \frac{1}{s + 3}$$ This transfer function appears stable because its only remaining pole lies at $s = -3$ in the OLHP. However, examining the system's differential equation with a non-zero initial condition $y(0) = y_0$ reveals that the unforced response contains the uncancelled natural mode: $$y(t) = \frac{y_0}{4}\left( e^{-3t} + e^{t} \right)$$ Because of the unstable exponential term $e^t$, any nonzero initial condition or minor disturbance forces the internal state and output to diverge toward infinity. In a unity feedback loop with plant $P(s) = \frac{b_p(s)}{a_p(s)}$ and controller $C(s) = \frac{b_c(s)}{a_c(s)}$, internal stability requires that all roots of the full characteristic polynomial: $$\Delta(s) = a_p(s)a_c(s) + b_p(s)b_c(s) = 0$$ lie strictly in the open left-half-plane. An unstable mode can only be stabilized by using feedback to shift the pole into the left-half-plane, not by mathematical cancellation.

## 2. Dynamic Geography of the Complex $s$-Plane

The location of closed-loop poles $s = \sigma \pm j\omega_d$ determines the natural modes that form the transient response:

```
                                    jω
                                     |
           Complex Conjugates:       |       Complex Conjugates:
           Decaying Oscillations     |       Growing Oscillations
                   x                 |                 x
                                     |
           Real Negative Axis:       |       Real Positive Axis:
           Pure Exponential Decay    |       Pure Exponential Growth
           <---- x ------- x --------+-------- x ------->  σ
                                     |
                   x                 |                 x
                                     |
```

### 2.1 Regional Characteristics of the $s$-Plane

1. **Left-Half-Plane (LHP, $\sigma < 0$)**:
    - Generates exponentially decaying modes $e^{\sigma t} = e^{-|\sigma|t}$.
    - Moving farther left (increasing $|\sigma|$) accelerates decay, shortens time constants ($\tau = 1/|\sigma|$), and reduces settling time ($T_s \approx 4/|\sigma|$).
2. **Right-Half-Plane (RHP, $\sigma > 0$)**:
    - Generates exponentially growing modes $e^{+\sigma t}$.
    - Any pole in the RHP makes the system unstable. Moving farther right accelerates divergence, causing early actuator saturation or physical damage.
3. **Imaginary Axis ($j\omega$-axis, $\sigma = 0$)**:
    - A single pole at the origin ($s = 0$) acts as an integrator, converting a step input into an unbounded ramp.
    - Simple, non-repeated conjugate pairs at $s = \pm j\omega_n$ yield sustained, undamped sinusoidal oscillations of constant amplitude $\cos(\omega_n t)$ (marginal stability).
    - Repeated (multiple) poles on the $j\omega$-axis produce modes of the form $t^n \cos(\omega t)$, which grow towards infinity over time and are strictly unstable.
4. **Vertical Motion Parallel to the $j\omega$-Axis (Varying $|\omega_d|$ at constant $\sigma$)**:
    - Increases the damped oscillation frequency $\omega_d = \omega_n \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}$.
    - Shortens peak time ($T_p = \pi/\omega_d$).
    - Decreases the damping ratio ($\zeta = \frac{|\sigma|}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \omega_d^2}}$), resulting in higher percentage overshoot and more oscillatory cycles before settling within the decay envelope set by $\sigma$.

## 3. Higher-Order Systems & Dominant Pole Approximations

Real engineering systems frequently contain three or more poles. Evaluating their transient response analytically is simplified by identifying whether a subset of these poles dominates system behaviour.

### 3.1 The Dominant Pole Concept

When an $n$-th order stable system is excited by a step input, partial fraction expansion yields: $$Y(s) = \frac{c_0}{s} + \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{c_i}{s - p_i} \implies y(t) = c_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i e^{p_i t}$$

- **Fast Poles**: Poles located far into the left-half-plane have large negative real parts (large $|\sigma|$). Their exponential terms $e^{-|\sigma|t}$ decay rapidly, becoming negligible early in the transient response.
- **Dominant Poles**: The poles closest to the imaginary axis (smallest $|\sigma|$) decay the slowest. These poles dominate the long-term transient response, dictating rise time, peak time, overshoot, and settling time.

### 3.2 The $5\times$ Rule-of-Thumb for Second-Order Approximation

A higher-order system can be accurately approximated by its dominant complex conjugate pole pair $s = -\zeta\omega_n \pm j\omega_d$ if:

1. **Pole Separation**: All additional real or complex poles lie **at least 5 times farther to the left** of the dominant poles: $$|\text{Re}(p_{\text{non-dominant}})| \ge 5 \cdot |\text{Re}(p_{\text{dominant}})| = 5 , \zeta\omega_n$$
2. **Absence of Uncancelled Zeros**: Any closed-loop zeros near the dominant poles will distort the response and must either be far into the left-half-plane or cancelled by nearby poles.

```
                 s-plane: 5x Separation Criterion
                               jω
                                |
               x (Far Pole)     |        x (Dominant Pole)
              -5ζωn             |       -ζωn + jωd
                                |
   <-------------|--------------+------------|------------ σ
               -5ζωn            |          -ζωn
                                |
                                |        x (Dominant Pole)
                                |       -ζωn - jωd
```

If these conditions are satisfied, standard second-order prototype formulas can be applied directly to estimate time-domain metrics: $$T_p \approx \frac{\pi}{\omega_d}, \qquad M_p \approx e^{-\frac{\zeta\pi}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}} \times 100\%, \qquad T_s \approx \frac{4}{\zeta\omega_n} ; (2\% \text{ criterion})$$

## 4. The Influence of Zeros on Transient Response

While poles determine the natural exponential modes of a system, zeros govern the modal amplitudes and can significantly reshape the transient response.

### 4.1 Addition of a Left-Half-Plane (LHP) Zero

Consider placing a real zero at $s = -z$ in a prototype transfer function: $$G_{\text{zero}}(s) = \left(1 + \frac{s}{z}\right) G_0(s) = G_0(s) + \frac{1}{z} s G_0(s)$$

Taking the inverse Laplace transform for a step input shows that the output response corresponds to the baseline response plus a scaled derivative of that response: $$y_{\text{zero}}(t) = y_0(t) + \frac{1}{z} \frac{dy_0(t)}{dt}$$

```
   y(t) ^
        |                  /---\  With LHP Zero (Faster rise, higher overshoot)
        |                 /     \
   1.0 -|----------------/-------\---------------------------------
        |              .' \       \
        |             /    '------- Baseline Second-Order Response y0(t)
        |            /
        |           /
        +----------+-----------------------------------------------> t
```

- **Speeding Up the Response**: Because the derivative $\frac{dy_0}{dt}$ is largest during the initial transient, adding a LHP zero increases early response velocity, reducing rise time ($T_r$).
- **Increased Overshoot**: The positive derivative adds to the response around the peak, which increases percentage overshoot ($M_p$) and can cause an otherwise overdamped system to exhibit overshoot.
- **Proximity Effect**: As the zero moves closer to the origin (smaller $z$), the derivative weighting $\frac{1}{z}$ increases, amplifying both response speed and overshoot. As $z \to \infty$, the zero's influence vanishes.

### 4.2 Right-Half-Plane (RHP) Zeros and Non-Minimum Phase Undershoot

A system with one or more zeros in the right-half-plane ($z > 0$) is designated a **non-minimum phase system**: $$G_{\text{RHP}}(s) = \left(1 - \frac{s}{z}\right) G_0(s) \implies y(t) = y_0(t) - \frac{1}{z} \frac{dy_0(t)}{dt}$$

```
   y(t) ^
        |
   1.0 -|---------------------------------------.------------------
        |                                      / \
        |                                     /   \
        |                                    /     '---------------
        +-----------------------------------/----------------------> t
        |       .-.                        /
        |      /   \                      /
        |     '     '--------------------'
        v   Undershoot (Inverse Response)
```

- **Inverse Response (Initial Undershoot)**: Because the derivative term is subtracted, the initial slope $\left.\frac{dy}{dt}\right|_{t=0^+}$ is negative for a positive step command. The output moves in the opposite direction to its commanded setpoint before recovering.
- **Physical Examples**:
    - _Aircraft Altitude_: Deflecting an elevator up creates a downward aerodynamic tail force that initially drops aircraft altitude before pitching the nose up to climb.
    - _Boiler Drum Water Level_: Injecting cold feedwater temporarily collapses steam bubbles, causing the liquid level to drop before heating increases volume.
- **Fundamental Design Limitation**: The undershoot and phase lag introduced by an RHP zero cannot be eliminated with feedback. Raising the controller gain forces closed-loop poles toward the RHP zero, driving the system toward instability. As a result, closed-loop bandwidth must be kept lower than the frequency of the RHP zero.

## 5. The Root Locus Method: Foundations & Construction Rules

Introduced by Walter R. Evans, the **Root Locus** is a graphical technique that plots the trajectories of all closed-loop poles on the complex $s$-plane as a single system parameter (typically loop gain $K$) varies from $0 \to \infty$.

```
     r(t)        +   e(t)       +-------+  u(t)   +-------+        y(t)
     ----------->(x)----------->| K C(s)|-------->|  P(s) |----+---->
                  - ^           +-------+         +-------+    |
                    |                                          |
                    +------------------------------------------+
```

### 5.1 Mathematical Formulation: Angle and Magnitude Conditions

The closed-loop transfer function under unity feedback is: $$T(s) = \frac{K G(s)}{1 + K G(s)}$$ The closed-loop poles are the roots of the characteristic equation: $$1 + K G(s) = 0 \iff K G(s) = -1$$

Expressing $-1$ in polar form as a complex number of unit magnitude and odd multiple angle: $$-1 = 1 \cdot e^{j(2k+1)\pi}, \quad k \in {0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots}$$

This yields two conditions:

1. **The Angle Condition (Determines Geometry)**: A point $s_0$ in the complex plane lies on the root locus **if and only if**: $$\angle G(s_0) = \sum_{j=1}^{m} \angle(s_0 - z_j) - \sum_{i=1}^{n} \angle(s_0 - p_i) = \pm 180^\circ(2k + 1)$$ The net sum of angles from all open-loop zeros minus the angles from all open-loop poles to the test point must equal an odd multiple of $180^\circ$.
2. **The Magnitude Condition (Determines Gain $K$)**: If a test point $s_0$ satisfies the angle condition, the specific gain $K$ required to place a closed-loop pole at $s_0$ is: $$K = \frac{1}{|G(s_0)|} = \frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n} |s_0 - p_i|}{\prod_{j=1}^{m} |s_0 - z_j|} = \frac{\text{Product of pole vector lengths}}{\text{Product of zero vector lengths}}$$

---

### 5.2 Systematic Construction Rules for Root Locus ($K \ge 0$)

#### Rule 1: Number of Branches

The root locus has $n$ continuous branches, where $n$ is the number of open-loop poles (order of $a(s)$). Each branch tracks the movement of a single closed-loop pole.

#### Rule 2: Starting ($K = 0$) and Ending ($K \to \infty$) Points

- As $K \to 0$, the characteristic equation $a(s) + K b(s) = 0$ reduces to $a(s) = 0$. Thus, **all $n$ branches originate at open-loop poles**.
- As $K \to \infty$, the equation approaches $b(s) = 0$. Thus, **$m$ branches terminate at the finite open-loop zeros**.
- The remaining $n - m$ branches travel toward infinity along straight-line asymptotes to balance the pole excess $n_{pe} = n - m$.

#### Rule 3: Symmetry

Because complex roots of real polynomials always occur in conjugate pairs, the root locus is **symmetrical with respect to the real $\sigma$-axis**.

#### Rule 4: Real-Axis Segments

A point on the real axis lies on the root locus **if and only if the total number of real open-loop poles and real open-loop zeros to its right is odd**. _(Points to the right of an even number of real poles and zeros produce an angle sum of $k \cdot 360^\circ$, failing the angle condition)._

#### Rule 5: Asymptotes for Large Gain ($s \to \infty$)

The $n - m$ branches heading to infinity follow straight-line asymptotes radiating from a single real-axis point:

- **Asymptote Centroid ($\sigma_a$)**: $$\sigma_a = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i - \sum_{j=1}^{m} z_j}{n - m} = \frac{\sum \text{Poles} - \sum \text{Zeros}}{n - m}$$
- **Asymptote Angles ($\theta_a$)**: $$\theta_a = \frac{(2k + 1)180^\circ}{n - m}, \quad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots, (n - m - 1)$$

|Pole Excess ($n - m$)|Number of Asymptotes|Asymptote Angles ($\theta_a$)|Geometric Shape|
|:-:|:-:|:-:|:--|
|**1**|1|$180^\circ$|Single negative real line|
|**2**|2|$\pm 90^\circ$|Vertical line parallel to $j\omega$-axis|
|**3**|3|$60^\circ, 180^\circ, 300^\circ$ (or $\pm 60^\circ, 180^\circ$)|Star radiating toward RHP|
|**4**|4|$\pm 45^\circ, \pm 135^\circ$|Crossed axes ($X$-pattern)|

#### Rule 6: Breakaway and Break-in Points

Where two or more closed-loop poles meet on the real axis and split into the complex plane (breakaway), or where complex branches return to the real axis (break-in):

- Along the real axis, gain can be expressed as $K(\sigma) = -\frac{a(\sigma)}{b(\sigma)}$.
- Breakaway points correspond to local extrema of gain along the real axis, calculated via: $$\frac{dK}{ds} = 0 \quad \text{or equivalently} \quad \sum_{j=1}^{m} \frac{1}{\sigma - z_j} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{\sigma - p_i}$$
- Only solutions $\sigma$ that lie on valid real-axis segments of the root locus represent physical breakaway/break-in coordinates.

#### Rule 7: Imaginary Axis ($j\omega$-Axis) Crossings

Points where the locus crosses the $j\omega$-axis define the boundary between stability and instability ($K_{\text{crit}}$ and oscillation frequency $\omega_{\text{osc}}$). They are calculated by:

1. **Routh-Hurwitz Stability Criterion**: Construct the Routh array for $\Delta(s) = a(s) + K b(s) = 0$ as a function of $K$. Find the critical gain $K_{\text{crit}}$ that creates a row of zeros (marginal stability), and solve the resulting auxiliary equation for $\pm j\omega_{\text{osc}}$.
2. **Direct Frequency Substitution**: Substitute $s = j\omega$ into $a(s) + K b(s) = 0$, separate into real and imaginary equations, and solve simultaneously for $K_{\text{crit}}$ and $\omega_{\text{osc}}$.

---

### 5.3 High-Gain Instability Mechanisms

The root locus illustrates two common causes of feedback loop destabilization:

1. **High Pole Excess ($n - m \ge 3$)**: When a system has three or more excess poles, the asymptote angles include directions pointing directly into the right-half-plane (e.g., $\theta_a = \pm 60^\circ$ for $n - m = 3$, and $\pm 45^\circ$ for $n - m = 4$). Consequently, as gain $K$ increases, at least two branches must eventually cross the imaginary axis into the RHP. Very high gain will therefore destabilize any plant with $n - m \ge 3$.
2. **Open-Loop RHP Zeros**: Because every open-loop zero serves as a termination target ($K \to \infty$), any zero in the RHP will pull a closed-loop pole branch across the $j\omega$-axis as gain increases.

## 6. Worked Root Locus Example

Consider a unity-feedback position control system with open-loop plant: $$G(s) = \frac{K}{s(s + 2)(s + 4)}$$

```
                            s-plane Root Locus
                                    jω
                                     |         / Asymptote (60°)
                                     |   x    /
                                     |  /    /
                         Breakaway   | /    /
     -4             -2     -0.88     |/    /
   ---x--------------x-------*-------x----+----> σ
   Open-Loop      Open-Loop          |   0
     Pole           Pole             |\
                                     | \    \
                                     |  \    \
                                     |   x    \
                                     |         \ Asymptote (300°)
```

1. **Poles and Zeros**: Three poles at $p_1 = 0$, $p_2 = -2$, $p_3 = -4$ ($n = 3$). No finite zeros ($m = 0$). Pole excess $n_{pe} = 3 - 0 = 3$.
    
2. **Real-Axis Segments**: Evaluating the number of poles to the right:
    
    - Between $0$ and $-2$: 1 pole to the right (odd) $\implies$ **Locus exists on $[-2, 0]$**.
    - Between $-2$ and $-4$: 2 poles to the right (even) $\implies$ No locus.
    - To the left of $-4$: 3 poles to the right (odd) $\implies$ **Locus exists on $(-\infty, -4]$**.
3. **Asymptotes**:
    
    - Centroid: $\sigma_a = \frac{0 + (-2) + (-4)}{3} = \frac{-6}{3} = -2.0$.
    - Angles: $\theta_a = \frac{(2k + 1)180^\circ}{3} \in {60^\circ, 180^\circ, 300^\circ}$.
4. **Breakaway Point**: Along the real axis segment between $0$ and $-2$: $$K = -s(s + 2)(s + 4) = -(s^3 + 6s^2 + 8s)$$ $$\frac{dK}{ds} = -(3s^2 + 12s + 8) = 0 \implies s = \frac{-12 \pm \sqrt{144 - 96}}{6} = -2 \pm \frac{\sqrt{48}}{6} \approx -2 \pm 1.155$$
    
    - $s_1 = -0.845$ (lies on $[-2, 0]$ $\implies$ **Valid breakaway point**).
    - $s_2 = -3.155$ (lies on $[-4, -2]$ $\implies$ Inactive).
5. **Imaginary Axis Crossing ($j\omega$-Crossing)**: Characteristic equation: $s^3 + 6s^2 + 8s + K = 0$. Forming the Routh array:
    
    - $s^3$: $1$, $8$
    - $s^2$: $6$, $K$
    - $s^1$: $\frac{48 - K}{6}$
    - $s^0$: $K$
    
    Setting the $s^1$ row to zero yields the critical stability gain: $$K_{\text{crit}} = 48$$ The auxiliary equation from the $s^2$ row gives: $$6s^2 + 48 = 0 \implies s^2 = -8 \implies s = \pm j\sqrt{8} \approx \pm j 2.83\text{ rad/s}$$ _Conclusion_: The system is stable for $0 < K < 48$. At $K = 48$, it oscillates continuously at $\omega = 2.83\text{ rad/s}$. For $K > 48$, two poles enter the RHP, causing instability.
