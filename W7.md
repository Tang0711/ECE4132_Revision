## 1. Foundations of Continuous-Time State-Space Models

### Revision Checklist

- [ ] **Define the state vector** $x(t)$ and explain why $n$ first-order ordinary differential equations (ODEs) can represent an $n$-th order physical system.
- [ ] **Distinguish between dimensions** of inputs ($p$), states ($n$), and outputs ($q$) for SISO and MIMO systems.
- [ ] **State the general continuous-time state-space equations** in matrix form and define each constituent matrix ($A, B, C, D$).
- [ ] **Explain the physical meaning of the direct feedthrough matrix** $D$ and identify when $D = 0$.

---

### Core Concepts & Formulations

#### State-Space Representation

A continuous-time linear state-space model with $p$ inputs, $q$ outputs, and $n$ state variables is expressed as: $$\dot{x}(t) = Ax(t) + Bu(t)$$ $$y(t) = Cx(t) + Du(t)$$

- **State Vector** $x(t) \in \mathbb{R}^n$: The minimal set of internal dynamic variables that completely summarize the past history of the system, enabling the exact prediction of its future state trajectory given future inputs. The dimension $n$ is the **order** of the system.
- **Input Vector** $u(t) \in \mathbb{R}^p$: The external driving signals or control inputs applied to the system actuators. If $p = 1$, the system is **single-input**; if $p > 1$, it is **multi-input**.
- **Output Vector** $y(t) \in \mathbb{R}^q$: The measured signals or variables of interest provided by system sensors. If $q = 1$, the system is **single-output**; if $p = q = 1$, the system is **single-input single-output (SISO)**; if $p, q > 1$, it is **multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO)**.
- **System (Dynamics) Matrix** $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$: Characterizes the internal coupling, autonomous interactions, and natural unforced modes among the state variables.
- **Input Matrix** $B \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$: Dictates how external control inputs enter and influence the state derivatives.
- **Output Matrix** $C \in \mathbb{R}^{q \times n}$: Maps the internal system states to the measurable output quantities.
- **Direct Transmission (Feedthrough) Matrix** $D \in \mathbb{R}^{q \times p}$: Represents an instantaneous, algebraic bypass path directly from the input to the output. In strictly proper physical systems where inputs cannot jump through to outputs with zero delay, $D = 0$.

## 2. Time-Domain Solutions & The Matrix Exponential

### Revision Checklist

- [ ] **Define the matrix exponential** $\exp(At)$ via its infinite power series and state its derivative property.
- [ ] **State the linear decomposition principle** separating the complete response into zero-input and zero-state solutions.
- [ ] **Derive the zero-input response** $x_{\text{zi}}(t)$ and verify initial condition satisfaction.
- [ ] **Formulate the zero-state response** $x_{\text{zs}}(t)$ as a matrix convolution integral.
- [ ] **Write down the full solution** for both the state vector $x(t)$ and output vector $y(t)$ for $t \ge 0$.

---

### Core Concepts & Formulations

#### The Matrix Exponential

For an $n \times n$ matrix $A$, the matrix exponential is defined by the globally convergent power series: $$\exp(At) = e^{At} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{t^k}{k!} A^k = I + At + \frac{A^2 t^2}{2!} + \frac{A^3 t^3}{3!} + \dots$$

Differentiating this series term-by-term with respect to time confirms the fundamental identity: $$\frac{d}{dt}\exp(At) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{k t^{k-1}}{k!} A^k = A \sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty} \frac{t^\ell}{\ell!} A^\ell = A \exp(At) = \exp(At)A$$

Furthermore, the matrix exponential satisfies the exponential composition law: $$\exp((t - \tau)A) = \exp(tA)\exp(-\tau A)$$ with $\exp(0) = I$.

#### Response Decomposition

Due to linearity, the overall state solution splits into two decoupled components: $$x(t) = x_{\text{zi}}(t) + x_{\text{zs}}(t)$$

1. **Zero-Input Response ($x_{\text{zi}}(t)$):** The response driven purely by the initial state $x(0) = x_0$ with no input applied ($u(t) = 0$): $$x_{\text{zi}}(t) = \exp(At)x_0, \quad t \ge 0$$ _Verification:_ Evaluating at $t = 0$ yields $x_{\text{zi}}(0) = \exp(0)x_0 = I x_0 = x_0$. Differentiating confirms $\dot{x}_{\text{zi}}(t) = A\exp(At)x_0 = Ax_{\text{zi}}(t)$.
    
2. **Zero-State Response ($x_{\text{zs}}(t)$):** The response starting from rest ($x_0 = 0$), driven purely by the input history $u(t)$ via the convolution integral: $$x_{\text{zs}}(t) = \int_{0}^{t} \exp(A(t - \tau))Bu(\tau) , d\tau, \quad t \ge 0$$ This represents the convolution of the matrix-valued impulse response kernel $\exp(At)B\mathbf{1}(t)$ with the vector-valued control signal $u(t)\mathbf{1}(t)$.
    

#### Complete Analytical Solution

Combining both contributions yields the total time-domain state trajectory and output trajectory: $$x(t) = \exp(At)x_0 + \int_{0}^{t} \exp(A(t - \tau))Bu(\tau) , d\tau$$ $$y(t) = C\exp(At)x_0 + \int_{0}^{t} C\exp(A(t - \tau))Bu(\tau) , d\tau + Du(t)$$

## 3. Transfer Functions & Frequency-Domain Realizations

### Revision Checklist

- [ ] **Derive the transfer function matrix** $G(s) = C(sI - A)^{-1}B + D$ by taking the Laplace transform of the state-space model.
- [ ] **Relate the resolvent matrix** $(sI - A)^{-1}$ to the Laplace transform of the matrix exponential $\exp(At)$.
- [ ] **Express $(sI - A)^{-1}$ using the adjugate and determinant** to analyze system poles.
- [ ] **Explain the relationship between the eigenvalues of $A$ and the poles of $G(s)$**, including the critical effect of pole-zero cancellations.

---

### Core Concepts & Formulations

#### Derivation of the Transfer Function

Applying the unilateral Laplace transform to the state-space equations with initial condition $x(0) = x_0$ yields: $$sX(s) - x_0 = AX(s) + BU(s) \implies (sI - A)X(s) = x_0 + BU(s)$$ $$X(s) = (sI - A)^{-1}x_0 + (sI - A)^{-1}BU(s)$$

Substituting $X(s)$ into the Laplace transform of the output equation $Y(s) = CX(s) + DU(s)$ gives: $$Y(s) = C(sI - A)^{-1}x_0 + \left[ C(sI - A)^{-1}B + D \right] U(s)$$

For zero initial conditions ($x_0 = 0$), the input-output relationship simplifies to the transfer function (or transfer matrix in MIMO systems): $$G(s) = \frac{Y(s)}{U(s)} = C(sI - A)^{-1}B + D$$

#### Laplace Transform of the Matrix Exponential

The matrix function $\exp(At)\mathbf{1}(t)$ has the Laplace transform: $$\mathcal{L}\{\exp(At)\mathbf{1}(t)\} = \int_{0}^{\infty} \exp(At)e^{-st} \, dt = (sI - A)^{-1}$$ which directly mirrors the scalar Laplace pair $\mathcal{L}{e^{at}\mathbf{1}(t)} = \frac{1}{s - a}$.

#### Poles vs. Eigenvalues

Using the matrix inverse identity $M^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(M)} \text{adj}(M)$, the transfer function can be written as: $$G(s) = \frac{1}{\det(sI - A)} C \text{adj}(sI - A) B + D = \frac{b(s)}{\det(sI - A)}$$ where each entry of $\text{adj}(sI - A)$ is a polynomial in $s$ of degree at most $n - 1$.

- **Eigenvalues of $A$:** The scalar roots of the characteristic equation $\det(sI - A) = 0$.
- **Poles of $G(s)$:** The singularities where $G(s) \to \infty$.
- **Core Rule:** **Every pole of $G(s)$ is an eigenvalue of $A$**. However, the converse does not always hold: if an internal state is uncoupled from either the input or the output, algebraic pole-zero cancellations occur between $b(s)$ and $\det(sI - A)$. Such cancelled eigenvalues remain internal natural modes of $A$ but disappear from the external transfer function $G(s)$.

## 4. Coordinate Transformations & Similarity

### Revision Checklist

- [ ] **Define a linear state coordinate transformation** using an invertible change-of-basis matrix $T$.
- [ ] **Derive the transformed system matrices** $(\tilde{A}, \tilde{B}, \tilde{C}, \tilde{D})$ in terms of $(A, B, C, D)$ and $T$.
- [ ] **List the system properties invariant under similarity transformations** (transfer function, eigenvalues, characteristic polynomial, reachability/observability).
- [ ] **Explain how diagonalizing $A$ decouples an $n$-dimensional system** into $n$ independent scalar first-order systems.

---

### Core Concepts & Formulations

#### Linear Change of State Variables

If a new state variable $\tilde{x}(t)$ is introduced via the invertible transformation matrix $T \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$: $$\tilde{x}(t) = T^{-1}x(t) \iff x(t) = T\tilde{x}(t)$$

Differentiating and substituting into the original system equations yields the equivalent transformed realization: $$\dot{\tilde{x}}(t) = T^{-1}\dot{x}(t) = T^{-1}(Ax(t) + Bu(t)) = (T^{-1}AT)\tilde{x}(t) + (T^{-1}B)u(t)$$ $$y(t) = C(T\tilde{x}(t)) + Du(t) = (CT)\tilde{x}(t) + Du(t)$$

Thus, the transformed realization matrices are: $$\tilde{A} = T^{-1}AT, \quad \tilde{B} = T^{-1}B, \quad \tilde{C} = CT, \quad \tilde{D} = D$$

#### System Invariants

Coordinate transformations merely change the mathematical perspective without altering the physical system dynamics:

1. **Identical Input-Output Response:** For any input $u(t)$, the output remains exactly the same: $y(t) = \tilde{y}(t)$.
2. **Identical Transfer Function:** $$\tilde{G}(s) = \tilde{C}(sI - \tilde{A})^{-1}\tilde{B} + \tilde{D} = (CT)(sI - T^{-1}AT)^{-1}(T^{-1}B) + D = C(sI - A)^{-1}B + D = G(s)$$
3. **Identical Characteristic Polynomial & Eigenvalues:** $$\det(sI - \tilde{A}) = \det(sI - T^{-1}AT) = \det(T^{-1}(sI - A)T) = \det(T^{-1})\det(sI - A)\det(T) = \det(sI - A)$$
4. **Preserved Structural Properties:** The system is reachable/observable in coordinates $x$ if and only if it is reachable/observable in coordinates $\tilde{x}$.

#### Diagonalization and Modal Decoupling

If $A$ has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors, it is diagonalizable. Constructing $T$ such that its columns are the eigenvectors of $A$ yields a diagonal matrix $\Lambda$ containing the eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$: $$T^{-1}AT = \Lambda = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_n)$$

Under this modal coordinate change, the state equation splits into $n$ decoupled scalar first-order differential equations: $$\dot{\tilde{x}}_i(t) = \lambda_i \tilde{x}_i(t) + \beta_i(t), \quad i = 1, 2, \dots, n$$ where $\beta_i(t)$ is the $i$-th element of $T^{-1}Bu(t)$. The scalar solution for each mode is: $$\tilde{x}_i(t) = e^{\lambda_i t}\tilde{x}_i(0) + \int_{0}^{t} e^{\lambda_i(t - \tau)}\beta_i(\tau) , \, d\tau$$ When eigenvalues appear as complex conjugate pairs $\lambda = \sigma \pm j\omega$, the corresponding decoupled modal states evolve as complex conjugate pairs. If $A$ possesses repeated eigenvalues lacking a full set of linearly independent eigenvectors, it cannot be diagonalized and is instead converted to **Jordan canonical form**, featuring eigenvalues along the main diagonal and 1s on the superdiagonal.

## 5. Canonical Realizations: Reachable Canonical Form

### Revision Checklist

- [ ] **Construct a state-space model in Reachable Canonical Form** directly from a given transfer function numerator and denominator.
- [ ] **Identify the companion matrix structure** in the $A$ matrix and the standard form of the $B$ vector.
- [ ] **Explain how the direct feedthrough scalar** $b_0$ modifies the $C$ and $D$ matrices when a transfer function is proper versus strictly proper.

---

### Core Concepts & Formulations

#### Construction of Reachable Canonical Form

Given an arbitrary proper transfer function of order $n$: $$G(s) = \frac{b_0 s^n + b_1 s^{n-1} + \dots + b_{n-1}s + b_n}{s^n + a_1 s^{n-1} + \dots + a_{n-1}s + a_n}$$

The state-space realization in **Reachable Canonical Form** (also known as Controller Canonical Form) is defined by: 
$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 
-a_1 & -a_2 & -a_3 & \dots & -a_{n-1} & -a_n \\ 
1 & 0 & 0 & \dots & 0 & 0 \\ 
0 & 1 & 0 & \dots & 0 & 0 \\ 
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \dots & \vdots & \vdots \\ 
0 & 0 & \dots & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 
0 & 0 & \dots & 0 & 1 & 0 
\end{bmatrix}, \quad 
B = \begin{bmatrix} 
1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \\ 0 
\end{bmatrix}$$

$$C = \begin{bmatrix} (b_1 - a_1 b_0) & (b_2 - a_2 b_0) & \dots & (b_{n-1} - a_{n-1} b_0) & (b_n - a_n b_0) \end{bmatrix}, \quad D = [b_0]$$

#### Strictly Proper Simplification ($b_0 = 0$)

When the transfer function is strictly proper (the degree of the numerator is strictly less than $n$), $b_0 = 0$, which simplifies $C$ and eliminates feedthrough: $$C = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 & b_2 & \dots & b_{n-1} & b_n \end{bmatrix}, \quad D = 0$$

#### Significance of Reachable Canonical Form

1. **Direct Characteristic Polynomial Mapping:** The first row of the companion matrix $A$ contains the negatives of the denominator coefficients $-a_1, -a_2, \dots, -a_n$, meaning its characteristic polynomial is immediately: $$\det(sI - A) = s^n + a_1 s^{n-1} + \dots + a_{n-1}s + a_n$$
2. **Controller Design:** State feedback control $u = -Kx$ applied to a system in this form alters only the first row of $(A - BK)$, making closed-loop pole placement transparent by directly shifting polynomial coefficients.

## 6. Stability in State Space: Internal vs. BIBO Stability

### Revision Checklist

- [ ] **Define internal stability** based on the zero-input state trajectory.
- [ ] **State the eigenvalue test for internal stability** ($\text{Re}(\lambda_i) < 0$).
- [ ] **Compare internal stability with BIBO stability** and explain why pole-zero cancellations can conceal internal instability.

---

### Core Concepts & Formulations

#### Definition of Internal Stability

A continuous-time linear state-space model $\dot{x}(t) = Ax(t) + Bu(t)$ is **internally stable** if its unforced zero-input trajectory converges asymptotically to the origin for every possible initial state: $$\lim_{t \to \infty} x_{\text{zi}}(t) = \lim_{t \to \infty} \exp(At)x(0) = 0, \quad \forall x(0) \in \mathbb{R}^n$$

#### Eigenvalue Criterion

A linear state-space system is **internally stable if and only if all eigenvalues of the system matrix $A$ have strictly negative real parts**: $$\text{Re}(\lambda_i(A)) < 0, \quad \forall i \in {1, 2, \dots, n}$$

#### Comparison: Internal Stability vs. BIBO Stability

- **BIBO Stability:** A transfer function $G(s)$ is Bounded-Input Bounded-Output (BIBO) stable if every bounded input signal produces a bounded output signal, which requires all **poles** of $G(s)$ to lie strictly in the open left-half complex plane ($\text{Re}(p_i) < 0$).
- **The Critical Discrepancy:** BIBO stability evaluates only the external input-to-output map. If an unstable mode (an eigenvalue of $A$ with $\text{Re}(\lambda) \ge 0$) is cancelled by an open-loop plant zero, that mode does not appear in $G(s)$. In this scenario, the transfer function appears BIBO stable, yet the physical system is **internally unstable**—the unmeasured internal state corresponding to the cancelled eigenvalue will grow without bound, leading to actuator saturation, component damage, or physical failure.

## 7. Nonlinear State-Space Systems & Linearization

### Revision Checklist

- [ ] **Write down the general nonlinear state-space equations** $\dot{x} = f(x, u)$ and $y = h(x, u)$.
- [ ] **Define an autonomous system** and an equilibrium point $(x_e, u_e)$.
- [ ] **Explain how Taylor series expansion yields the Jacobian matrices** $A$ and $B$ for small perturbations around an operating point.

---

### Core Concepts & Formulations

#### Nonlinear Formulation

Physical dynamic processes often exhibit nonlinear behavior governed by coupled nonlinear ODEs: $$\dot{x}(t) = f(x(t), u(t)), \quad y(t) = h(x(t), u(t))$$ where $f: \mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}^n$ and $h: \mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}^q$ are vector-valued functions. When the system receives no external input, or has constant inputs, or operates under feedback where $u$ is purely a function of state, the system is described as **autonomous**: $\dot{x} = f(x)$.

#### Equilibrium Points & Linearization

An **equilibrium point** $(x_e, u_e)$ is a state-input pair where the state derivatives vanish: $$f(x_e, u_e) = 0$$

Defining small perturbations around this operating point: $$\delta x(t) = x(t) - x_e, \quad \delta u(t) = u(t) - u_e, \quad \delta y(t) = y(t) - y_e$$ Taking a first-order Taylor series expansion yields the standard linearized model: $$\dot{\delta x}(t) \approx A \delta x(t) + B \delta u(t)$$ $$\delta y(t) \approx C \delta x(t) + D \delta u(t)$$ where the matrices are evaluated at the equilibrium point as the Jacobians: $$A = \left. \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \right|_{(x_e, u_e)}, \quad B = \left. \frac{\partial f}{\partial u} \right|_{(x_e, u_e)}, \quad C = \left. \frac{\partial h}{\partial x} \right|_{(x_e, u_e)}, \quad D = \left. \frac{\partial h}{\partial u} \right|_{(x_e, u_e)}$$
